Costs and ROI

How to measure the ROI of a cleaning robot in your hotel

Method and reproducible example to measure the ROI of a cleaning robot in a hotel: hours freed up, realisable savings, operating costs and payback for purchase or a monthly-fee service.

15 June 2026 · Updated 12 September 2026 · A practical read for hospitality and facility management

Why measure before buying

An autonomous cleaning robot shouldn't be evaluated by its purchase price alone. In a hotel, the return appears when you measure hours freed up, service stability, fewer incidents and the ability to cover slots where staff are more expensive or harder to organise. That's why at Alpha Robotics we recommend starting with an operational assessment and defining indicators before deploying the robot. This article explains the method and applies it to an example with visible assumptions that you can replace with your own figures.

Three figures you should not mix up

Hours freed up: the manual working time the robot covers. Value of the freed-up capacity: those hours multiplied by the fully loaded labour cost; it is capacity the team can devote to rooms or guest service, not cash coming in. Realisable monetary saving: the share of that value that turns into lower real spending (shifts not covered, overtime or outsourcing avoided). For example, 2.5 h/day × 26 days = 65 h/month of freed-up work; that does not automatically mean cutting 65 hours of payroll.

The metrics that matter

Area cleaned per shift, staff hours spent on common areas, recorded incidents, the cost of night shifts and compliance with cleaning audits. In hotels of 100 to 250 rooms, the greatest potential is usually in the lobby, floor corridors, spa areas, event areas and high-traffic spaces that require repetitive routines.

Sensitivity to the realisable saving factor (purchase): at 25% (€635/month net) payback stretches to ≈21 months; at 100% (€3,470/month) it drops to ≈4 months. That is why the factor must be set with your real shifts, not a generic value.

Which costs to always include

Investment and deployment (mapping, training, accessories), maintenance and support, energy and consumables, the team's supervision time, incidents and, if you contract a service, the fee. For a purchase, payback is the initial investment divided by the monthly net saving, provided it is positive. For a monthly-fee service, compare the operating benefit with the costs of the contracted period, with no initial investment. Ownership, maintenance and continuity conditions depend on the contracted option.

Six months: evaluating is not recovering

Six months is a good period to evaluate with real data (a 2–4 week pilot plus follow-up), not a payback promise. In the example, the investment is recovered in about nine months with a 50% saving factor; it only drops below six months if most of the hours covered were actually paid hours, such as outsourced night shifts. Your figure comes from your shifts, your floor area and your cost per hour.

How to set up a pilot

A measurable pilot should last between 2 and 4 weeks. During that period you compare times, routes, incidents and the team's perception, and you set the three figures of the method: hours freed up, capacity reassigned and realisable saving. The goal isn't to replace everyone but to free staff from repetitive tasks so they can focus on points where human judgement adds the most value.

Conclusion

ROI is calculated with real operating data and explicit assumptions, not generic promises.
